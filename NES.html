Slide 16: The 3.5-Inch Timeline & The "High Density" Hole Visual Idea: A picture of a standard 720KB disk next to a 1.44MB disk, with big arrows pointing to the extra "High Density" square hole punched in the top left corner. 1982–1984: The Beginning (400 KB) Sony invented the 3.5-inch format in 1982, but it took off when Steve Jobs chose it for the original 1984 Macintosh. The Tech: These early disks were "Single-Sided, Double-Density" (SSDD). The drive head only read one side of the magnetic tape inside. They held about 400 Kilobytes (Mac) or 360 Kilobytes (PC). 1986: Flipping the Disk (720 KB / 800 KB) The Tech: Engineers realized they were wasting half the magnetic material. They upgraded the disk drives to have two reading heads—one on top, one on the bottom—so they could read both sides of the spinning disk simultaneously. This created "Double-Sided, Double-Density" (DSDD) disks, immediately doubling storage to 720 KB (PC) or 800 KB (Mac). 1987: The Global Standard (1.44 MB) The Tech: Manufacturers changed the chemical makeup of the magnetic coating, packing the magnetic particles much closer together. This created the "High Density" (HD) disk. The Physical Change: To tell the computer that a disk was High Density, manufacturers punched a second square hole in the plastic shell opposite the write-protect switch. The drive had a physical pin that poked through that hole to confirm it could write 1.44 Megabytes of data. This specific 1.44 MB disk remained the global standard well into the 2000s. 1991: The Extended Density Flop (2.88 MB) IBM tried to push "Extended Density" disks that held 2.88 MB. The Reality: They were incredibly expensive to manufacture, and game sizes were already exploding past 10 or 20 Megabytes. The 2.88 MB disk failed completely because the industry realized that magnetic disks were at their absolute physical limit, paving the way for the laser-read CD-ROM. Talking Point: "If you look closely at a 3.5-inch disk from the 1990s, you will see two little square holes at the top corners. One was a slider to 'lock' the disk so you couldn't accidentally erase your homework. The other was literally just a hole. That empty space was a physical message to the computer drive saying, 'I have high-grade magnetic tape inside, you can squeeze 1.44 Megabytes onto me!' Today, a single picture taken on your smartphone takes up about 3 to 4 Megabytes. You would need three of these physical disks just to save one photo."
